# Punk / Zine

## Signature
photocopy grain, ransom-note type, torn paper, marker scrawls, stamps,
confrontational layout
`--noise: 1;`

## Suited for
music, activism, streetwear, independent publishing, events, countercultural
brands

## Layout
assemble pages like photocopied manifestos or gig flyers; diagonal fragments,
pasted headlines
forceful top-to-bottom reading route

## Colors
- high-contrast black and white with urgent red or fluorescent marker accents;
  visible toner
  and paper variation
- primary: urgent red
- secondary: fluorescent marker yellow or green
- base-100: dirty white
- base-200: newsprint gray
- base-300: darker toner gray

## Imagery
direct-flash band or street photography, photocopied portraits, protest
ephemera, safety pins
tape, stamps
hand-cut graphics

## Density
dense visual layering and compressed statements; dates, venues, prices,
and actions separated into unmistakable blocks

## Motion
cut-and-paste entrances, marker scribbles, jittered photocopy frames, stamp
impacts
fast rough wipes tied to content changes

## Shape
torn paper, taped corners, ransom-note blocks, rough stars, stencil marks,
thick scratched dividers
`--radius-*` set to 0 or 0.25rem

## Typography
mix marker, stencil, ransom-note, and condensed display lettering for slogans
hard-working mono or grotesque for logistics and body copy
Suggested: Special Elite, Rock Salt, Permanent Marker, Black Ops One, Rubik Dirt

## Origins
mid-1970s–1980s, with continuing independent-print influence; punk scenes in
New York, London
and other cities
zines arose as cheap, fast, participant-made media alongside punk music;
photocopying, typewriters
ransom-note lettering, hand marks, collage,
and deliberately rough reproduction documented scenes outside established
publishing
later alternative magazines, grunge,
and web anti-design retained that do-it-yourself visual politics
Sources: vam.ac.uk/articles/the-art-of-punk-and-the-punk-aesthetic,
bl.uk/events/punk-1976-78
