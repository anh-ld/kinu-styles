# Glitch Art

## Signature
pixel sorting, compression artifacts, signal tearing, channel shifts, scanlines
corrupted imagery; `--noise: 1;`

## Suited for
music, games, security, technology campaigns, experimental media, digital-art
portfolios

## Layout
start from a stable digital grid; interrupt selected images, headings
or boundaries with localized signal failure while core navigation remains intact

## Colors
- dark or neutral foundations with separated RGB channels, compression blocks
  electric error colors, occasional blown signal white
- primary: electric cyan
- secondary: channel-shift magenta or red
- base-100: neutral charcoal
- base-200: near-black
- base-300: signal black

## Imagery
portraits, screens, landscapes, or product media transformed through pixel
sorting, datamosh
tearing, scanlines
and codec artifacts

## Density
medium; corruption limited to focal zones so users distinguish intentional
artifact from missing
or broken content

## Motion
brief channel shifts, frame tears, buffer skips,
and pixel displacement on transitions or system events
then restore a stable readable state

## Shape
sliced rectangles, displaced bands, broken scan fields, pixel blocks
misaligned channels derived from digital signal structure; `--radius-*` set
to 0 or 0.25rem

## Typography
neutral sans or mono for functional text
distortion only on duplicated decorative layers or large headings with an
intact readable copy
Suggested: Rubik Glitch, Rubik Glitch Pop, Major Mono Display, VT323, Share
Tech Mono

## Origins
1960s electronic-art precedents; named digital practice from the late
1990s–2000s; video art
sound art, software
and network culture; Nam June Paik's manipulated television work in the 1960s
later artists exposed compression errors, broken codecs, corrupted files,
circuit bends,
and software failure
Rosa Menkman's writing and events helped establish glitch as a critical
digital practice rather
than a generic RGB effect
