# Halftone / Comic Book

## Signature
halftone dots, ink outlines, speech balloons, panel frames, action bursts,
punchy colors
`--noise: 1;`

## Suited for
entertainment, sports, youth campaigns, games, events, narrative landing pages

## Layout
interface story told through ordered panels and gutters; one splash panel for
the dominant moment
smaller frames for supporting beats

## Colors
- primary: punchy print red or blue
- secondary: yellow or cyan
- base-100: white paper
- base-200: warm newsprint
- base-300: light halftone gray
- texture: `--noise: 1;`
- black ink with a limited print palette; adjacent fills clearly separated
  halftone screens for secondary tones

## Imagery
inked illustrations, action photography converted to dots, close-up character
crops
sequential scenes with strong focal expressions

## Density
medium-to-high narrative density; copy divided into short captions
and balloons so no panel becomes a wall of text

## Motion
snap between panels, expand action bursts, draw speed lines, reveal captions
in paced beats
do not move every halftone texture

## Shape
heavy panel frames, speech and thought balloons, jagged bursts, speed wedges,
thick contour lines
`--radius-*` set to `0` or `0.25rem`

## Typography
expressive comic display lettering paired with a highly legible dialogue
face; compact balloon text
uppercase only where the genre calls for impact
Suggested: Bangers, Luckiest Guy, Comic Neue, Lilita One, Anton

## Origins
commercial printing from the late 19th century; comic-book prominence in the
20th
Origin: industrial print shops in Europe and the United States
Halftone processes simulated tonal images with fields of dots
and became practical in mass print during the late 19th century
Benjamin Day's related line-and-dot methods date to 1879; cheap color comics
made dot screens
flat separations, heavy ink, and registration quirks culturally familiar
Pop artists later enlarged those marks into subjects of their own
