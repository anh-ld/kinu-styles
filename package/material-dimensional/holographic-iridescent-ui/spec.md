# Holographic / Iridescent UI

## Signature
iridescent gradients, spectral color shifts, pearlescent surfaces,
diffraction highlights
reflective foil
prismatic glow

## Suited for
beauty, fashion, music, Web3, premium technology, futuristic branding

## Layout
dark or neutral editorial foundation; foil surfaces isolated in hero objects,
badges, dividers
and selected controls so each spectral moment feels intentional

## Colors
- angle-shifting cyan, violet, rose, and lime across pearlescent highlights
- grounded by charcoal, white, or soft gray fields
- token mapping: `primary` = cyan-violet, `secondary` = rose or restrained lime
  `base-100`/`base-200`/`base-300` = white, pearl gray,
  and soft cool gray or tightly stepped charcoals
- `--depth: 1`

## Imagery
macro shots of iridescent film, reflective packaging, translucent
three-dimensional forms
prismatic light captured against simple backgrounds

## Density
low to moderate; dark breathing room around reflective focal elements
no rainbow treatment on every component

## Motion
spectral shifts tied to pointer angle, scroll position, or object rotation
slow shimmer passes rather than constant random hue cycling

## Shape
smooth discs and inflated foil membranes mixed with a few faceted prisms
functional containers kept simpler than decorative objects; `--radius-*`
between 0.75rem and 1.5rem

## Typography
wide futuristic display type for short statements, paired with a neutral
sans-serif body
iridescent fills restricted to large readable letterforms
Suggested: Syncopate, Michroma, Oxanium, Space Grotesk, Zen Dots

## Origins
2010s–present as a UI label; optical roots in the 20th century; digital
fashion, music
motion graphics
and interface design; a current visual label rather than direct holography
— most screens imitate iridescence with animated gradients and specular color
vocabulary borrows from physical holography (invented by Dennis Gabor
1947) and diffraction films and metallic fashion of the late 2010s
Sources: nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1971/summary
